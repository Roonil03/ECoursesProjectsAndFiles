First-Person POV:
The order came through the rain: raise your rifle and fire.

I was standing in the western line with mud inside my boots and a loose button scratching my neck. Across the square, villagers pressed against the bakery wall. Some held their hands over their heads; others held children close. None of them looked like enemies.

“Steady!” the sergeant shouted.

My rifle felt heavier than it had that morning. I tried to focus on the cracked fountain behind the crowd, but a little girl in a red scarf stepped into my sight. She was holding a wooden horse with one wheel missing.

The first volley cracked across the square.

People fell. The sound struck my chest harder than the recoil. Smoke rolled between us and the wall, turning every face into a pale shape.

I fired because everyone beside me fired.

The girl remained standing. Her mother pulled her down, but the scarf caught on a broken stone and stretched between them. The mother tugged once, twice. I saw the little horse drop into the mud.

“Fire again!”

My finger rested against the trigger.

For one breath, I imagined lowering the rifle. I imagined the sergeant shouting my name, the soldiers behind me shooting through my back, and my own mother waiting for a letter that would never arrive.

I fired.

The red scarf disappeared beneath the smoke.

When the square became silent, the sergeant walked past us, checking our weapons. My rifle had jammed open, its barrel smoking.

“Reload,” he said.

I looked at the empty bakery, the shattered fountain, and the wooden horse lying in the mud.

My hands moved automatically.

I had been trained to obey orders, but no one had taught me what to do with the silence afterward.


Omniscient POV:
Rain washed the square without cleaning it.

The villagers stood beneath the bakery’s awning, crowded so tightly that shoulders touched shoulders. A mother held her daughter against her skirt, while the girl clutched a wooden horse missing one wheel. Around the square, soldiers raised their rifles.

Among them stood Tomas, an ordinary infantryman with mud in his boots and a loose button scratching his neck. He had been told that the villagers were collaborators, though no evidence had been shown to him. The sergeant had been told only that the order came from above. Somewhere beyond the square, the commander waited beside a covered truck, already thinking about the next village.

“Fire!”

The first volley broke the morning apart.

Bullets struck the bakery wall and the people beneath it. The mother fell first, though her fingers remained twisted in the child’s red scarf. The girl stumbled free and stared at the soldiers, still holding the wooden horse.

Tomas fired with the others. He did not see the baker’s apprentice collapse behind the doorway, nor the old man who died with his hands raised. He saw only the girl.

The sergeant shouted for a second volley.

Tomas’s finger hesitated. His fear was not for himself alone; he imagined the soldiers behind him, the punishment for disobedience, and the mother who believed he was still safe somewhere far from the war. He did not imagine the girl’s mother had once sung lullabies while kneading bread, or that the girl had named the wooden horse Thunder.

The rifles fired again.

The girl vanished into smoke. Her red scarf remained caught on the stones, bright against the gray mud.

When the shooting stopped, rain tapped against helmets and pooled in the footprints of the dead. The sergeant inspected the soldiers’ weapons. Tomas’s rifle hung open, smoking.

“Reload,” the sergeant ordered.

Tomas obeyed. The commander climbed into the truck. The surviving soldiers formed ranks. No one noticed the wooden horse until the wind rolled it beneath the bakery door.

There, its single wheel turned once, then stopped.


Comment on the POV Shift:

The first-person version is narrower and more intimate because it focuses only on Tomas’s fear, hesitation, and guilt, while the omniscient version expands the scene by revealing the villagers’ identities, the commander’s detachment, and the broader consequences of the massacre; the first-person perspective creates uncertainty, whereas the omniscient perspective creates a wider moral judgment.
